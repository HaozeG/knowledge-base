# EUV and High-NA Lithography for Advanced Semiconductor Manufacturing

## Overview

Extreme ultraviolet (EUV) lithography uses 13.5 nm wavelength light to print features on silicon wafers. Standard EUV (0.33 NA) has been the workhorse for 7nm through 3nm nodes. High-NA EUV (0.55 NA) is the next step, enabling 8nm single-exposure resolution — necessary for 2nm-class nodes and below. The lithography tool is the most expensive and physically constrained piece of semiconductor manufacturing equipment, and ASML is the sole supplier of EUV systems, making lithography a structural bottleneck for AI chip production.

- [supported][src:asml-high-na-euv-2025] High-NA EUV (EXE:5200B) achieves 8nm resolution with 0.55 numerical aperture, ~175 wafers/hour throughput, and 0.7nm overlay accuracy. Each system costs ~$380–400M (vs. $150–200M for standard EUV). The tool weighs ~150 tons and requires 3–4 Boeing 747 cargo planes to transport.
- [supported][src:trendforce-high-na-adoption-2026] Customer adoption is bifurcated: Intel is the most aggressive adopter (EXE:5200B qualified December 2025 for 14A 1.4nm node), Samsung uses High-NA for both logic and DRAM, SK hynix for advanced DRAM. TSMC is notably not adopting High-NA for its A14 (1.4nm) node, instead extending 0.33NA EUV with multi-patterning — a cost-optimization bet that exploits its existing tool amortization.
- [inference] The lithography tool is the ultimate pacing item for Moore's Law: one EUV layer requires one pass through one lithography tool. A modern 3nm chip has ~20–25 EUV layers. At 175 wafers/hour, one EXE:5200B produces ~1.5 million wafer-layers per year. With ~100 EXE:5200B tools expected by 2028, total High-NA capacity is ~150 million wafer-layers/year — enough for ~6–8 million advanced AI chips annually. This hard capacity ceiling is the physical origin of the "advanced node shortage."

## EUV Technology

### How EUV Works

- [inference] EUV light at 13.5nm is generated by vaporizing tin droplets with a CO₂ laser, creating a plasma that emits EUV radiation. The light is collected by multilayer mirrors (Mo/Si, ~40% reflectivity per mirror) and passed through a reflective mask that defines the pattern. Because EUV is absorbed by air (and by glass lenses), the entire optical path operates in vacuum with reflective (not transmissive) optics.
- [inference] The 0.33 → 0.55 NA transition is not a simple lens upgrade. Higher NA increases the angle of light incident on the wafer, which reduces depth of focus (the vertical range over which the image stays sharp). At 0.55 NA with current resist thickness (~30–50nm), depth of focus is ~20–30nm — requiring atomic-scale planarization of each preceding layer. This is the primary manufacturing challenge at 2nm and below.

### Multi-Patterning vs. High-NA

- [inference] Standard EUV (0.33 NA) can resolve ~13nm features in a single exposure. To achieve the 8–10nm features needed at 2nm, manufacturers have two options: (1) multi-patterning with 0.33 NA (LELE: litho-etch-litho-etch, doubling the number of exposures per layer), or (2) single-exposure with High-NA (0.55 NA). Multi-patterning doubles the number of lithography steps, increasing cost and defect risk proportionally. High-NA reduces patterning steps by 20–35% for critical layers but doubles the tool cost.
- [inference] TSMC's bet on extending 0.33 NA: by optimizing multi-patterning with existing amortized tools, TSMC avoids the $400M-per-tool CapEx of High-NA while still achieving 2nm-class features. Intel's bet on High-NA: by adopting the new tool first, Intel reduces per-wafer patterning complexity and potentially achieves better yields at 1.4nm. Both bets have precedent in semiconductor history (the shift from 193nm dry to 193nm immersion lithography followed a similar pattern in 2004–2007).

## Supply Chain Dynamics

- [supported][src:wedbush-high-na-monopoly-2026] ASML has a monopoly on EUV lithography — no other company produces EUV tools. The company's order backlog is ~€38.8 billion, reflecting demand that far exceeds production capacity. ASML ships ~50–60 EUV systems per year (standard + High-NA combined). This creates a zero-sum allocation dynamic: every High-NA tool shipped to Intel is one less tool available for Samsung or TSMC (if they order).
- [inference] The tool allocation is as strategic as the technology itself. Intel's aggressive High-NA orders (first in line) give it a temporary process advantage at 1.4nm if TSMC's multi-patterning approach hits yield issues. Conversely, if TSMC's 0.33 NA multi-patterning succeeds at competitive yields, Intel's $400M-per-tool investment may not translate to cost-competitive wafers. The 2026–2028 timeframe will resolve this bet.

## Physical Scaling Limits

- [inference] Each lithography generation pushes closer to fundamental physical limits. At 3nm features (~12 silicon atoms across), line edge roughness (LER) — random variation in the edge position of a printed line — becomes a significant fraction of the feature size. LER is fundamentally limited by photon shot noise: fewer photons define smaller features, increasing statistical variation. High-NA's larger collection angle helps by capturing more photons per feature, but the statistical limit is irreducible.
- [inference] Beyond High-NA lies Hyper-NA (0.75 NA), targeting ~2030 for sub-0.5nm equivalent nodes. At these dimensions, quantum tunneling between adjacent features and atomic-scale roughness dominate variability. The semiconductor industry is approaching the point where lithography resolution improvements no longer translate to transistor density improvements — the end of Moore's Law from a patterning perspective.

## Open Questions

- OPEN: Will TSMC's bet on extending 0.33 NA EUV with multi-patterning for A14 succeed at competitive yields, or will Intel's first-mover High-NA advantage translate to a genuine process leadership position for the first time since 2018?
- OPEN: At what node does multi-patterning complexity (3× or 4× patterning) make High-NA economically inevitable regardless of tool cost? Most analysts place this crossover at 1.4nm.
- VERIFY: High-NA's 175 wafers/hour throughput is ASML's specification; real-world throughput at customer fabs (Intel, Samsung) may differ due to resist sensitivity, dose requirements, and tool availability.
